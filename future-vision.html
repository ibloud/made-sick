FUTURE VISION · 2035–2040 THESIS

The future should adapt to people.
Not the other way around.

Made Sick explores what happens when technology is designed around human capacity instead of demanding that humans continuously adapt themselves to technology.

Read the moonshot See the roadmap ↗ Inspect the evidence ↗

The moonshot

Make participation accessible by default.

The goal is not to build a prettier platform. It is to test whether creative and civic technology can reduce avoidable cognitive friction while preserving identity, ownership, consent, and agency.

01

Human capacity first

Creative tools can respond to cognitive diversity without requiring surveillance, diagnosis, or a person to repeatedly explain their needs.

02

Identity follows the creator

A creator's identity and relationships should not become disposable whenever a platform changes.

03

Consent travels with the work

Publication, discovery, contact, collaboration, and reuse should have clear permission boundaries instead of relying on ambiguity.

04

Access is infrastructure

Accessibility should be an acceptance criterion for systems, not a late-stage feature request.

05

Information becomes usable

Public information—including civic resources—should be easier to understand and reach without requiring institutional expertise.

06

AI serves agency

Automation can assist people without silently taking authorship, identity, consent, or decision-making away from them.

Why this exists

It started at a crossroads in Minneapolis.

Made Sick did not begin as a conventional product plan. It began with a deeply personal vision: trying to help a sick boi find meaning and purpose, while finding meaning and purpose in my own life again.

Every step since then has required confronting a fear—uncertainty, failure, visibility, responsibility, technology, and the possibility that something that mattered deeply to me might not matter to anyone else.

I kept going anyway.

Why stop now?

The title and mood of the movie Why Stop Now? fit this chapter of the experience: a crossroads, a difficult road, and the decision to keep moving rather than abandon a vision because it became frightening.

What began as one personal act of trying to restore meaning became an experiment in building something useful for many people: a place where creativity, accessibility, identity, consent, civic participation, and technology can meet without asking people to surrender themselves to the system.

This is an account of the creator's experience and motivation. It does not claim that any person named in the project's public references endorsed, joined, funded, advised, or partnered with Made Sick.

Prototype → proof

Small today. Testable tomorrow.

Made Sick should not ask anyone to believe that the whole thesis is already proven. The current work is a set of concrete experiments that can be challenged, tested, and improved.

PIXIE Device Stewardship

A consent-first research hypothesis about avoidable cognitive labor in operating systems and creative tools, with an interaction prototype rather than a diagnostic or health service.

Creator-owned identity

Experiments with AT Protocol identity and public writing aim to keep the creator at the center of identity and publication decisions.

Story Finder

A device-local research interface for public Bluesky feeds. It does not use passwords, private messages, or private records.

Civic access

The voting-resource flow is a concrete accessibility experiment: reduce friction between a person and authoritative voting information without turning the project into a political organization.

The path

A roadmap from prototype to evidence.

NOW

Build the foundation

Static prototype, consent architecture, PIXIE research, creator identity experiments, Story Finder, and concrete accessibility flows.

NEXT

Test with creators

Participatory stories, accessibility review, stronger AT Protocol integration, and documented user research with real workflows.

PILOT

Measure friction

Test whether the proposed interaction patterns actually reduce cognitive effort, improve clarity, or preserve agency compared with ordinary workflows.

SCALE

Open the method

Publish methodology, findings, failures, and interoperable patterns so others can reproduce or challenge the work.

LONG HORIZON

Change the default

Explore whether human-centered participation infrastructure can work across creative disciplines and civic contexts without centralizing ownership of the people using it.

What we need from the future

Don't just believe it. Stress-test it.

Creators

Use the system. Tell us where it adds friction instead of removing it.

Researchers

Challenge the assumptions and help design measurements that could falsify the thesis.

Accessibility experts

Find the places where “accessible” language still produces inaccessible behavior.

Technologists

Help make identity, permissions, portability, and privacy interoperable rather than proprietary.

Filmmakers & storytellers

Show what a humane technology future could feel like before it exists.

People with lived experience

Identify the quiet failures that technical teams routinely miss.

The case for Made Sick

One screen. One argument.

Problem: Technology increasingly asks humans to adapt themselves to systems that were not designed around cognitive diversity.

Why now: AI, automation, decentralized identity, and increasingly digital civic and creative participation are changing how people work, publish, and make decisions.

What's different: Made Sick treats consent, identity, accessibility, and creator ownership as infrastructure rather than decoration.

What exists: PIXIE research, creator-identity experiments, Story Finder, voting-resource accessibility work, and a public prototype.

If it works: participation becomes easier without requiring people to surrender agency.
