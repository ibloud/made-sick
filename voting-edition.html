Learn before you vote

Understand the decision. Check the evidence.

Start with the office or question on your own ballot, then compare evidence using the same questions for everyone. These are reading guides and lesson materials, not a calendar of booked classes. No resource here decides your vote for you.

1. Understand the office or ballot question

Minnesota Secretary of State: How Elections Work explains election types, administration and ballot questions.

Official Minnesota reference. Rules and responsibilities differ elsewhere.

Try this: find your official sample ballot, choose one office and check its legal powers. For a ballot measure, read the exact wording and any official fiscal analysis; identify what a yes or no vote changes.

2. Compare candidates fairly

League of Women Voters: How to Judge a Candidate offers seven steps for evaluating positions, experience and leadership beyond slogans.

VOTE411 provides nonpartisan candidate questionnaires and ballot information where local coverage is available. Candidates’ answers are their own statements, not independently established facts. No response is not a position.

Independent nonprofit voter education, not an election authority or a Made Sick partner. Address-based lookup happens on VOTE411 under its own privacy terms.

Try this: ask each candidate the same question. Compare the proposed action, evidence, cost, legal authority and past record; note unanswered questions.

3. Check sources before sharing

Elections Canada: Digital Skills for Democracy provides classroom materials about evaluating online information for civic decisions.

Canadian civic-education lesson, adaptable for group learning. The publisher reports that materials are being updated; check the current version and intended audience. This is not a confirmed adult class booking.

Try this: trace a claim to its original source. Check the date, jurisdiction, evidence and who paid for the message; distinguish reporting, opinion and paid campaigning. Cross-check important claims instead of judging by popularity.

4. Participate between elections

Minnesota Legislature: May I testify at a committee hearing? explains arranging testimony with committee staff.

UK Parliament: Give evidence to a select committee explains contributing to an inquiry. Check its subject, submission deadline and publication terms.

Official participation guides. Legislative committees, local public hearings and general public comment have different rules.

Use the hearing preparation checklist below. For a city, county or school-board matter, start with that body’s official agenda and participation instructions.

Which decisions affect what you care about?

No election is universally the most important. Start with an issue, identify who has authority and compare the choices available on your ballot. These U.S. examples are starting points, not a substitute for your jurisdiction’s laws; local bodies often share responsibilities with state or federal government.

Schools and education
School boards and school ballot measures can affect district policy, budgets and facilities; state lawmakers and agencies also set rules and funding.
Housing, planning and development
City councils, county government and relevant planning bodies may handle zoning, development and local housing policy. State law can limit or change their powers. Some planning bodies are appointed rather than elected.
Services, taxes and public spending
Local boards and councils, state legislators and relevant executive offices make different budget and implementation decisions. Check which body actually controls the service and funding.
Courts and justice
Judges interpret and apply law; legislators make law, and executive officials administer it. Where judges appear on a ballot, examine qualifications, jurisdiction, ethics and the applicable selection rules rather than treating the office as another legislative seat.

Try this: write down one concern and one question for the responsible office. Check a candidate’s promise against what that office can legally do. Use the official election resources above to confirm which offices and questions actually appear on your ballot.

Prepare for a public hearing

Watching, submitting written evidence and speaking are different ways to participate. An open meeting does not automatically offer unrestricted speaking time. A formal hearing may address a specific proposal; a general public-comment period may have a different scope.

  1. Find the responsible body. Use its official website to locate the agenda, meeting notice, proposal or bill number, staff report and supporting documents. Check whether the matter is a hearing, a discussion or a decision.
  2. Read the participation rules. Confirm the date, time and timezone, location or remote access, registration and written-submission deadlines, speaking limits and any topic restrictions. Ask the clerk or committee staff if instructions are unclear.
  3. Request access support. Ask about accessible formats, interpretation, captions, remote participation and accommodation deadlines. Availability and procedures vary; request confirmation from the body.
  4. Prepare a short, factual statement. Identify the proposal, explain the effect or evidence you want considered, and make a specific request or ask a clear question. Separate personal experience, verified evidence and interpretation; link supporting sources.
  5. Check privacy before submitting. Comments, contact details and recordings may become public records. Read the publication rules and avoid unnecessary medical, financial, address or other private information.
  6. Follow the result. Check the minutes, recording, amendments, vote and next meeting. Participation does not guarantee a particular decision.

Made Sick does not collect testimony, register you to speak or submit comments. Prepare and submit through the responsible body’s own channel, after reviewing what will be public.
